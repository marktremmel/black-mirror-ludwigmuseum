Black Mirror. The Long Shadow of the Future
Thematic Selection from the Ludwig Museum’s Collection

“The future is too dark, so don’t stay here with me!” – FO. System
 
The Ludwig Museum’s collection, which has been developing and expanding for several decades, features a number of contemporary works that deal with imaginary visions of the future or interpret the present as a dystopian reflection of the past in some way. These are artistic thought experiments that paint a dark picture of the possible future or present, connecting the current and the future in time, offering a glimpse into the future of humanity. The dystopian idea, as one of the extreme values of possible realities, is also a model, a point of reference, the contemplation of which is natural and necessary for understanding actual reality, and in this sense it is both a tool and a method.
 
As the first stage of a longer-term research project, the exhibition examines the works in the collection through the concept of dystopia and includes other works by artists featured in the collection in this exploration. The selected works represent the discourse that has been taking place recently, primarily in the Hungarian art scene, which the exhibition analyzes along various thematic paths. This phase serves as a preparatory stage for a broader inquiry, laying the groundwork for a more comprehensive analysis of our present, current interpretations of the concept of dystopia, and contemporary artistic visions of the future.
 
In addition to statements about visions of the future and possible conditions of the world, the exhibition also asks: “Has it not become colder?” (F. Nietzsche, The Gay Science)
 
Dystopia is a fictional representation of the world that presents negative developments in the present or future, usually through totalitarian, technocratic, or dehumanising structures. The central element of the dystopian worldview is social oppression, which involves control mechanisms at the collective level and limited freedom, loss of identity, and moral dilemmas at the individual level.
 
The concept of dystopia, which can be interpreted as a separate category in the history of ideas, is usually defined in relation to utopia (anti-utopia), but it is also subject to strong conceptual relativism. In contrast to utopia, which is the guiding idea of an ideal society, dystopia describes a radically opposite world, where the fictional world is always worse than the existing one. Dystopia as anti-utopia serves primarily as a (dark) mirror: the conditions and characteristics of the world it depicts amplify the negative aspects of the observer’s world. By magnifying our inner fears, it helps us understand what kind of future we want to avoid. Dystopia thus offers a radical critique of current social trends, a conceptual mapping of the crisis symptoms of modernity, which is also a source of anxiety.

A. THE SADNESS OF FULFILLED TALES

In today’s post-utopian world, the dream of a better future that once loomed on the horizon seems to have evaporated. In literature and film, the genre of science fiction made huge strides in the last decades of the 20th century, permeating everyday life, not least thanks to the achievements of the scientific and technological revolution. Within this genre, dystopia, whether as anti-utopia or negative utopia, has clearly earned a place for itself. In Hungary, the Kozmosz Fantasztikus Könyvek (Cosmos Fantastic Books) series sought to bring the sci-fi genre, which was otherwise labelled as children’s literature by the socialist party leadership and therefore considered frivolous, into the mainstream. This is how, a few years after their publication in their original language, numerous works from a wide spectrum of world literature were translated, which, in a closed socialist system, were a real intellectual nourishment for the construction of unique worlds or even escapism, not least thanks to the high quality of the translations.
 
The section entitled The Sadness of Fulfilled Tales is like a time loop in which the present, past, and future converge—as if the present were a black mirror reflecting an entirely non-fictional image of the future for the past. The exponentially accelerated scientific and technological developments that are changing our present at an unparalleled pace, coupled with the transformation of economic systems and processes, create an intriguing situation in light of the vivid dystopias that have emerged in recent decades: looking into it, our present appears to be a black mirror of the past, depicting oppressive premonitions and bad scenarios as reality rather than fiction. The concrete political manifestations of dystopia generate grim realisations: have we always lived in a dystopia? Further questions: does every dystopia start out as a utopia and inevitably end as an anti-utopia? Are dystopias proof of the impossibility of utopias, or are we talking about two fundamentally different worlds?
 
This thematic section bears the melancholy of the day after. It is the bittersweet recognition that the grand narratives have come to an end, or at least that we cannot find the way to them. Behind the concepts and their signifiers, instructive works and practices appear in the section: they document how dynamics may emerge in the background that result in a world conceived as utopian turning into a dystopia in reality, just as the past, turning towards the future, surrenders to the present.

Sai, OLEKSIY: Mildew (2018)
installation

The new installation continues a series of works on the subject of the so-called “office monkeys”, that is, the lowest caste of workers in the office hierarchy. The crowds of impersonal people wearing grey each day reproduce the monotonous routine of clerks’ life. “Mildew” – this is how this category of people is called contemptuously in Ukraine. However, at the same time these people become a great force in the moments of social turbulences. That was the case in Ukraine in 2004, and the same happened in 2014. Ukrainian middle class threw off their ties and went out on the streets. In spring of 2014 somebody literally went straight out from the office to war in the East of the country. However, revolutions and wars end, and impersonal men and women with constant smiles on their faces return to their usual way of life. Like the real mildew, they expand everywhere where there is an opportunity to deploy workspaces, distributing, as an epidemic, their specific way of life and thinking. And only sometimes, at the ritual Friday parties, the fuzzy images of other times when grey little people were able to make history appear.

DROZDIK Orshi: Adventure in Technos Dystopium (1983 in progress)
selection from the subsections of the series

The selection of works from the unit Adventure in Technos Dystopium presented in the exhibition offers a wide spectrum from Orshi Dorzdik’s period of the early 1980s through photographs, similias and other fabricated objects, as well as love letters. Orshi Drozdik belongs to the second generation of the Hungarian neo-avant-garde movement and was member of the Rózsa Circle, which played a key role in the art of the ‘70s. Since her early years as student at the Academy of Fine Art, much inspired by László Moholy-Nagy’s theories, her technical experiments in the field of photography with montages, cameraless photography and photograms occupied a central place in her oeuvre, examining the female roles, the biological, social and political aspects of identity and body. In the mid-1970s, she focused her resistance against the academic status quo through her experimental work: having drawn female nudes for long years, she turned reluctant to the masculinist corporeal academic regime. She formulated her response stating the emancipatory point of view of the woman. Having studied language and linguistics before art, her semiotic approach is also a crucial part of her overall practice. She moved from Hungary in 1978 to eventually, after several stops, settle in New York in 1981. It is during these years that she developed a longer and larger unit, Adventure in Technos Dystopium, which unfolded into several parallel adventures (series) addressing the historical formation of desires, knowledge and bodies. The title is explicitly distorted: she transformed techné, a feminine noun into technos a lexically non-existent term and neutered dystopia: “dystopia, i.e. negative technological utopia, refers to my adventures and disillusionment with the male scientific world,” writes Drozdik. “For women, neither a scientific language nor a discourse is ever a neutral ‘given.’ [...] The innocence and optimism of eighteenth century science marks the beginning of modern utopia. Since then, this utopia has disintegrated like missing layers of ozone in the atmosphere, leaving us with technology that consumes Nature.” She articulated her disappointment as a “woman chronicler” by writing love letters 
“in the same way that love letters are often written when illusions are fading”, some of which are here included. Continuing on an international scale a line of research started in Budapest, she entered deserted museums where she photographed documents and objects relating to 18th and 19th century scientific experiments. The black and white photos and other objects (i.e. similias) of the series Dystopium Infinite were grouped into subcategories: technology, the body/anatomy, pathology, nature... Meanwhile, she was engaged in a politically active New York 
based group Colab, and wanted to extend the form of classic conceptual art that she used for Dystopium Infinite, she created a fictional scientific woman, naming this fold of works “The Life of Edith Simpson Epitome of the Enlightenment, Rational Genius of Art and Science”. This invented but very much plausible person of whom she wrote a fake biography: Edith worked in the 18th century with static electricity and magnetism, dividing the world into two matters, salt and coal (originally, the Black Mirror works were exhibited with polished anthracites, i.e. black, reflecting cubes).
Drozdik aimed at literally incorporating the female entity – body, soul and 
mind – into the scientific discourse, disrupting the linear patriarchal evolution of the field and hence all domains that were to be impacted by science – the everyday narrative our physical and chemical world since the Enlightenment. “I accepted every word of patriarchal science as reality and the truth until such ‘truth’ turned into disillusionment as I began to recognize my position and my deprived state in this relationship (and in wider discourse)”. Building up a massive amount of objects (data, images, similias), collected or created, she aims to deconstruct and retrace an alternate narrative in the field of science where the rational is completed with the sensual and a psychological insight, questioning also scientific visualisation. Hence through the self, she approaches the structure, through her materials reflects on the distopical use of the instruments “vitrinising” the narrative. (KB)

Gentian SHKURTI: Alice in Wonderland, 1997
colour video with sound, English subtitle, 3’35”

In 1997 Albania underwent an economic and social crash: the market economy introduced in the mid-1990s, after almost 50 years of isolation and communist dictatorship, had soon turned into financial chaos on account of the government-supported pyramid scheme projects. The population took to the streets and plundered military bases, took arms, resulting in a quasi civil war. The state television almost entirely ignored the clashes—people were at best informed by international 
channels about the extent of the disorder.

This absurd situation inspired Shkurti to use Disney’s Alice in Wonderland in his video. Shkurti used two videotapes for the montage: by mixing the cartoon with news footages of international channels, he allows a new understanding of “Wonderland.” (KB)

KERESZTES Zsófia: Combat Car II., 2022
foam, structural adhesive, glass mosaics, grouting material, anchored steel, rolling cart, anchored metal chain

The work was part of a larger installation created by Zsófia Keresztes for her exhibition entitled After Dreams: I Dare to Defy the Damage for the 59th Venice Biennale in the Hungarian Pavilion. The mosaic sculpture installation, consisting of amorphous body fragments representing the stages of the search for identity, depicted the uncertainties of our time, our sense of life, and the ambivalent relationship of past and present to the future. Keresztes also calls her sculptures 21st century totems.
Her hybrid creature in a rolling container cart is at once menacing and vulnerable, at once sensual and frightening. The cold blue teardrops hanging from spider web-like metal chains — fossilised remnants of pain and pleasure — seem like archaic props from a post-digital world after dreams. 
The Venice installation also included a work titled Solitary Confinement, in which a teardrop resembling the tip of a spear appears magnified as a solitary sculpture within an organic, protective frame. The chains threaded through the frame simultaneously signify a desire for security and connection, for intertwining, but also dependence and the possibility of disintegration. (BG)

KIKINTERA: Postnaturalia & Supernaturalia, series 2018—2024

Krištof Kintera graduated from the Academy of Fine Arts in Prague in 1996. A multidisciplinary artist, he creates sculptures and installations using everyday objects, most of which are site-specific works. He frequently incorporates electric components into his works, such as motors, diodes, and switches. Kintera’s works reflect the uncertainties experienced by residents of former socialist countries in their 
daily lives as a result of rapid economic and social changes.

Several works from two different, however interconnected series appear here: Postnaturalia and Supernaturalia dates back to about a decade when Kintera started experimenting with electric garbage. The heart-breaking amount—mountains—of waste that he came across prompted him to create huge installations of imagined circuits, building a post-natural environment, a new geological layer of a landscape, composed of once preciously engineered, microscopic elements. 
Inspired by shifts in scale, his works reflect humanity’s dominion over electrons: Kintera uses ready-made objects as corpus delicti, so to speak, but he also treats them with fire or other methods to bring forth the blossoms of the new postAnthropocene era. 
Drawing on the methods and prototypes of earlier scientists—and in parallel with these designs—Kintera began experimenting with a homemade high-voltage device he had received as a gift, which bore the name “Lichtenberg.” This is how Kintera became acquainted with the work of Georg Christoph Lichtenberg, the German physicist who discovered the branching discharge patterns (Lichtenberg 
figures). In his experiments, Kintera uses two high-voltage conductors to create floral patterns, in which energy finds its own path to the surface, like lightning in the sky, a plant’s roots in the soil, or a river in the earth. Kintera’s method is a new form of landscape painting that examines a profound change that has occurred in reality—the fact that our everyday experiences no longer belong exclusively to the natural world. SZACSVA Y Pál: Reprojection XXXIV, 2002
C-print mounted on plastic cardboard, 95 × 165 cm

When approximately 30 years ago, Szacsva y Pál began producing his photographic series Reprojection, the works struck viewers with their conceptualism and striking execution. Large-scale, staged photography entered the Hungarian art scene in the early to mid 1990s and besides producing a substantial and impressive body of work, it also facilitated the introduction of the discourse of representation into Hungarian art theory.
For the generation of young artists emerging from art school around the time of the political transition, photography offered an 
effective means of articulating new ideas and provided them with new modes of producing art within the shifting political, social, and artistic conditions. The process underlying the Reprojection series is relatively straightforward. Szacsva y used his own photographs, mostly depicting banal scenes and mundane objects, and projected them with a slide projector onto various found surfaces in darkened interiors: a pile of clothes at home, an attic with scrap materials at a residency programme, and other similar environments.
This method produced what Freud called the ‘uncanny’: images in which it often becomes difficult to distinguish between the layers or to determine their individual sources. In certain cases, the combination of these seemingly neutral layers unexpectedly yielded socially critical meanings. Although such content may not always have been the artist’s primary intention, it emerged through chance encounters between the projected images and the material surfaces.

Since its creation in 2002, the interpretative possibilities of Reprojection XXXIV have shifted significantly. The work appears to have lost its initial art historical and theoretical context, gaining instead new layers of meaning. The way in which the image disorients the viewers by the sudden and unexpected switches between scales, generates a sense of unease that extends beyond the image itself. Today, having been exposed to an overwhelming number of images of catastrophe, war, 
destruction, and the materialization of negative scenarios, all are instantly and incessantly disseminated through social media, it becomes nearly impossible not to associate Reprojection XIII and XXXIV with the spectre of future disasters, new conflicts, and further ruptures.  It is as if the unfolding of a negative utopia appears increasingly difficult to avoid. (TK)

B. PERSONAL WORLDS

Dystopia is traditionally an imaginary reflection of our collective fears and social anxieties: imagined futures that anticipate the conflicts of the present. In this section, the emphasis shifts: grand narratives are replaced by individual perspectives, personal visions of the future, and the inner space of the creative self. The works on display do not outline a single, universal outcome, but rather a variety of possible futures existing in parallel—each through the perspective of a single creative consciousness.
 
Personal Worlds brings together contemporary artworks that explore utopias and dystopias in which creators build worlds from their personal experiences, intuitions, and doubts. These visions of the future are often fragmented, uncertain, and open-ended: they raise questions about social coexistence, the role of technology, the environmental crisis, and the transformation of identity, while not ruling out the possibility of individual responsibility and action.
 
In the works presented, global issues—climate change, social inequalities, surveillance/inspection, alienation—are closely intertwined with the position of the individual. The self appears in these futures sometimes as an observer, sometimes as a victim, and sometimes as an active agent. The artists do not view dystopia from the outside: they live in it, are part of it, shape it, or suffer from it. The emphasis on personal position shifts dystopia from mere warning toward self-reflection.
 
These works do not offer exclusively dark visions. In many cases, they also suggest alternatives, transitional states, fragile utopias—possible escape routes, new forms of coexistence, concrete tools and practices, personal strategies for survival or resistance. In the works of Personal Worlds, the situations and characters that appear alongside dystopian visions do not necessarily offer the promise of a sure solution, but rather maintain the idea of possibility: the chance that the future/present is not just a series of inevitable outcomes, but a shared space that can be constantly renegotiated and rewritten. Through contemporary visual art, Personal Worlds thus examines the role of individual responsibility, imagination, and decision-making in the process of constituting the future.

BORSOS Lőrinc: For the Child of the Future (Slingshot, Amazon, Baby Santa, Herczegh, Vitalis, Copy), 2023

The artist duo presented their project of the same title in 2023 at the Művészet Malom, part of the Ferenczy Museum in Szentendre. In comparison to the creative strategies that have characterized their work so far, the solo exhibition entitled 
For the Child of the Future, can be seen as a turning point in their oeuvre. After their initial, mainly politically and socially critical works, followed by a self-mythologising and self-analytical tone, while their current works have been marked by more experimental world-building strategies that can also be related to video and role-playing games. 
The work on view aim to create similar speculative realities. In addition to the darker aesthetics associated with the artists, known as the “nocturnal side of painting,” they also strive to establish a new, more informal, and experimental artistic language. The selection from the first part of this complex installation offers a glimpse into the magical rites and props of a techno-tribe existing beyond our world, which initially makes it hard to determine whether it was constructed for the children of the future or if they built it for us. 

The project of the Lőrinc Borsos artist duo can be interpreted as taking their aesthetic and psychological self-examination, which has been ongoing for many years, to a new level. It is an aesthetic endeavor to create a cross-generational community spirit whose aim is to collectively envision a post-apocalyptic world. However, the purpose of these works is not primarily to evoke visions of the end of the 
world, but rather to convey the dialectical tension between opposing elements. In other words: the depiction of a transcendental tension that arises in the lives of the artists but can also be interpreted from an aesthetic perspective, oscillating between the contradictory qualities of the present world and the afterlife, home and the distant, togetherness and separation, uniqueness and commonality, while symbolising the transition between speculative world-building and the total destruction of the world. These gestures may be just as recognizable in literary 
discourses of narratology, video game theories, as well as the worlds of theater, or LARP (live-action role-playing). This is an imagined scenario in which images of potential antecedents and conceivable consequences appear simultaneously. Thus, the elements of the installation carry forward the line of thought of the art 
philosopher Arthur C. Danto, who says that art, and the institutional practices associated with it, possess a transformative power that is capable of transforming experiences of mundane reality into extraordinary ones. (Excerpt from the curatorial text of Zsolt Miklósvölgyi)

GELLÉR B. István: Find From the Growing City (Maquettes for Foggy Nights), 1993
fire clay ceramics, copper, coloured photographs on paper, wire

The central project of István B. Gellér’s oeuvre, developed over many decades beginning in the early 1970s, is “The Growing City”, which can be interpreted as a reconstruction of a fictional yet intricately elaborated civilisation. The work is not a single piece but a self-referential system, a network of interconnected objects, models, drawings, texts, and pseudo-documents that creates the memory of an imaginary culture.
The project’s fundamental gesture lies at the intersection of archaeology and art. B. Gellér does not excavate an existing past, but creates the ruins of a “couldhave-been” civilisation (fictional archaeology), as if they were the remnants of a lost world. The fictional researchers, excavation notes, and seemingly scientific descriptions associated with the works construct an alternative knowledge system in which document and imagination merge.
“The Growing City” is not a static structure, but a constantly expanding, spirally organised system whose development proceeds according to its own internal laws. The model of the city simultaneously refers to archaic cultures and an imagined future: it is built upon the convergence of geometric and organic elements, while 
being imbued with layers of mythological and cosmological meaning. In the project’s world view, space is not merely a physical expanse, but a symbolic system in which life, destruction, and transcendent forces intertwine.
One of the key concepts of the works is fragmentation. The city is never shown in its entirety, but is revealed in the form of artifacts, fragments, and reconstructions. This absence functions not as a loss, but as a force that generates meaning: the viewer is compelled to fill in the missing parts imaginatively. With this method, B. Gellér also examines the nature of historical knowledge, pointing out that every construction of the past is a narrative and interpretive act.
“The Growing City” can be viewed both as a visual mythology and a critical reflection on the fragmented experience of modernity. The architectural forms, sacred objects, and textual fragments presented in the project depict an image of a culture that carries the promise of totality, while remaining accessible only in fragments. In this sense, Bruno Gellér’s work is not merely the story of an imaginary city, but also a metaphor for contemporary cultural experience: a world in which the whole becomes conceivable only through its parts. (KJ)

KASZÁS Tamás: Sci Fi Agit Prop (The Science Fiction of Agitative Propaganda) series, 2010—2018
selection from the poster series (14 pcs)

Science fiction, an apocalyptic atmosphere, prepper preparedness, and communist propaganda converge in the artist’s works—displayed on bulletin boards and created using diverse techniques—which have taken on new functions. The posters, as future (sci-fi) versions of agitprop, serve as messengers in a post-apocalyptic world. Kaszás envisions communication using simple found objects and materials: he builds from what he finds (or would find) in his surroundings in this situation. Modest in his means, didactic in his forms, he aims to help those in 
need and inspire survivors in an unspecified future through his posters, which often evoke the propaganda materials of constructivism or dictatorships. In their appearance, which often suggests an obvious lack of resources and incompleteness, the drawings appropriate the representational styles of earlier historical periods and transform them into tools of agitation and activism (serving good causes here and now). In the posters, art takes on a direct form; they do not serve aesthetic contemplation but aid survival: they bring to life the avant-garde idea of shaping society and the community through artistic means. The poster (as a descendant of Mayakovsky’s so-called “Rosta windows”) is at 
once a newspaper, an advertising space, an information bank for survival, and a user manual—for the children of later generations. The bulletin board was a well-known “interior design” element in factories, schools, and community centers during the decades of socialism. A public forum for public service messages, a well-established venue for communication, it served its purpose through its very practicality (a grid of simple slats, with a raw or varnished surface, covered with papers pinned in place with thumbtacks and pins). 
By using the bulletin board format, Kaszás recycles, reworks, and reinterprets the symbols and signs that propaganda widely disseminated. His method does not merely consist of mixing representational systems and visual forms, but also highlights the subjective element that is inevitably inherent in the creative process.
“(…) Each of these graphic works depicts various scenarios for life in the future following the collapse of modern civilization, brought about by worsening ecological and economic catastrophes occurring in parallel with the ideological collapse. These scenarios encompass both the mobilization of the creative and emancipatory power of future societies—aimed at mastering new practices of autonomous and self-sufficient living as well as forms of collective cooperation—and the struggle for survival. Kaszás uses inexpensive and easily accessible materials (e.g., linoleum, woodcuts, recycled paper) and simple techniques 
(cutting or screen printing). These methods draw on the visual codes and traditional reproductive tools of activist materials, such as the rapidly produced propaganda prints of the 1920s. According to the artist, one day—in the event of the destruction of high-tech infrastructure or shortages—these techniques may prove useful once again.” (Joanna Sokolowska)

Oto HUDEC: Wave in Front of the Garden, 2019
HD video, 3’39”

Oto Hudec is a multimedia artist, who creates videos, murals, animations, sculptures, sound pieces and works for public spaces about immigration, refugees and the effects of globalization on the environment. His projects often include a utopian perspective as a way to understand the production of food and energy; he tracks transformations of the industrial landscape or the decline of bees. His research on the topics of climate change, and ecology is often focused, instead on new scientific solutions, on how nomadic and indigenous people achieved sustainability. He often collaborates on projects with children and youth from disadvantaged communities. 
Situated in a dystopian future, there are few people who have survived the consequences of climate change. They are those, who created safe, protected environments, with enough supplies, water source, who escaped forest fires and floods. The air is hardly breathable due to large dust particles. To create a healthy environment for humans, it is necessary to create a closed biosphere, where the air people breathe comes from a small garden isconnectedattached to the air people breathe inside of their houses. This physically recreates once lost connection between natureal world and the human world. To survive the harsh conditions, 
we have become part of the garden, while the garden has becomes part of us. “This is how it started: The insects disappeared first. If you went out to a meadow in summer, you felt as if something was missing there. At first, no one would have guessed what. It was just somehow quieter. The flowers were also low. Winters were shorter and stranger, summers dry and hot. It all took place gradually, little by little, and people got used to it.“ 
The project follows the story of a man and a child — a small girl — who live in the future after climate crisis lonely, unsure if the rest of civilization still exist. Since nationalist ideology appropriates the motifs of national myths and origin stories, the HUF’s goal is to reclaim and reinterpret them. In this speculative universe, elements of prehistory, the Turul myth, shamanistic traditions, and folk art ornamentation are interwoven with cyborg bodies, alternative timelines, space travel, and posthuman forms of existence. In the imaginary cosmology of Hungarofuturism, the Hungarian people are not a peripheral, traumatized historical community, but an intergalactic entity that, by reprogramming its own past, attempts to devise a survival strategy in a collapsing world.
The language of the Hungarofuturist Manifesto is at once apocalyptic and ironic, heroic and parodic; blending the pathos of national romanticism with the rhetoric of techno-utopias. Part of this is the Hungarofuturist Pantheon, which reinterprets figures of Hungarian culture, politics, and art in an anachronistic manner. C. THE END OF IDEOLOGY

Ideology (a set of beliefs, social consciousness): a system of coherent ideas, views, values, and concepts professed by
a given social group (class, party, stratum) that describes and interprets the world (worldview) and formulates goals and operating principles for society, justifying or criticising the existing power relations.
 
The first half of the 20th century was not only fifty-years of dictatorships, but also the heyday of militant ideologies. Although utopia and dystopia are genres originating in literature, they are nevertheless ideas relating to the organisation of human society. Our historical experience shows that dystopia is not the realm of speculative fiction, but a very tangible everyday reality. In the wake of the past, the future has also become uncertain, with at least the looming threat of a dehumanized world.
 
The term the end of ideology comes from Daniel Bell’s 1960 essay of the same name. According to the author, the great ideologies of the 20th century (communism, fascism, liberalism, conservatism, etc.) are weakening, losing their appeal, and being replaced by pragmatism, postmodern relativism, populism, technocracy, and everyday, non-ideological political actions and identity politics, resulting in a more confusing and less transparent political world. The collapse of large-scale, comprehensive ideological systems is due, on the one hand, to the negative aspects of their implementation (socialism, capitalism) (utopia – dystopia) and, on the other hand, to the unforeseeable effects of social change.
 
The end of ideologies can first be recognized by its symptoms of crisis: the loss of grand narratives, the inability of a given ideology to comprehensively describe and interpret the functioning, motivations, and goals of society or its individual parts. The end of ideologies can also be understood as the dystopian realisation of social utopias (ideologies) that once set out to improve the world.
 
In this region of Europe, we may have plenty of personal or historical experience of the dominance of ideologies and their sudden collapse. The period closest to us in time, permeated by ideology, is the decades of socialism, which defined a phase in the lives of many artists. Consequently, a significant number of works in the museum’s collection and in the exhibition focus on the issue of the recent decline of ideology (communism/socialism, Marxism, dialectical materialism...) and its lifeworld. The works examine the ideologies of the era from different perspectives, mostly interpreting them beyond the framework of memory. Evoking symbols of the past and empty signs, they examine the validity of ideologies, their past and their possible future.

D. NO(N) FUTURE!

French anthropologist Marc Augé captured one of the fundamental experiences of space use in late modern societies with his concept of non-places (non-lieux). According to his definition, non-places are spaces that have no identity-based, historical, or relational significance: they are transitory, functional, and impersonal. As spaces of transportation, consumption, and administration, they are pivotal locations of everyday life, yet they lack everything that makes a ‘place’ meaningful in Augé’s interpretation. They convey a sense of alienation, anonymity, and timelessness, while being fundamental infrastructural elements of the functioning of global society.
 
The works in this section examine the concepts of place and non-place in the context of dystopia. The dystopian potential of non-places lies in the fact that, as the antithesis of places, they are social tropes of alienation, homelessness, surveillance, observation, and discomfort.
 
The presence of the individual in these spaces is temporary and regulated: movement is controlled, identity is reduced, behaviour is prescribed. The works often reinforce this state, shifting non-places toward total spaces of the future, where personal memory and attachment are permanently dissolved in the logic of function and system. At the same time, the No(n) Future! section does not exclusively convey the narrative of loss. Several works also question whether non-places are truly devoid of meaning or whether they simply offer different types of experiences.
 
There are works that attempt to reinterpret, appropriate, and transform non-places into temporary ‘places,’ raising the question of whether it is possible to create personal or collective meaning in late modern spatial structures. They approach the exploration of dystopian non-places and the presentation of their characteristics from the perspective of public space, memory, and cultural topology.
 
The works in this section thus offer a critical examination of the spatial experience of the future. Dystopia here is not necessarily a distant, imagined world, but an exaggerated, radicalized version of the spaces of the present. No(n) Future! draws attention to the fact that the social experience of the future is not only a temporal but also a fundamentally spatial question: it examines where—and under what conditions—the individual can become present in the world again.

E. METROPOLIS

The title of the section is a clear reference to Fritz Lang’s iconic film, Metropolis (1927), the most significant cinematic dystopia of the silent film era. After Metropolis, nothing was the same as before: the future of humanity (which until then had mostly been a source of hope) now revealed something fundamentally frightening, something unusual. Hope was replaced by something frightening in its inscrutability, and it did not take long for it to become relevant in times of totalitarian dictatorships and social oppression. Metropolis was like a bad premonition.
 
Etymologically, utopia refers to a “place that does not exist” (u-topos) (not to be confused with “non-place,” to which
a separate chapter of the exhibition is devoted) – a fictional place characterized by perfection. Utopian urban concepts have existed for centuries, dreaming up countless fictional spaces and structures, while also inspiring the structure of many real cities. The quest to bring wellbeing to society through city architecture reached its peak at the beginning of the 20th century with, among other things, Le Corbusier’s urban concepts: certain architectural movements, such as constructivism, were explicitly inspired by utopia, but as this chapter reflects, radical utopias can also become their own dystopias, burying even their most rigid supporting structures.
 
Human interaction with the immediate built environment becomes a defining element in narratives imagining a fictional future, especially when the examination of coexistence in large cities/crowded environments takes on a more radical, critical, and satirical approach within the framework of dystopia. In general, the social structures that appear in dystopias are governed by strong technological control, so the issue of human freedom plays a prominent role in fictional narratives. The human desire to dominate their environment as much as possible in order to satisfy their own needs evokes a better age in the case of a utopian city, but in a dystopian reading, it leads to the destruction of the environment. In addition to technological dehumanisation, one of the most drastic images is that of human subordination and vulnerability in an environment of their own making, where the elements take control and human freedom falls into its own trap.
 
The built environment is a direct reflection of social structures, and vice versa: spaces designed to facilitate coexistence may seem ideal on paper and offer solutions to a variety of problems, but in reality, over the decades, they can turn into a complete social nightmare. The disposal of urban spaces, the memory and stratification of spaces, and the personal narratives associated with them appear in the works in this section.

Andreas FOGARASI: Haus der Begegnung ( House of Encounters) architectural model, 2019
architectural model, cardboard, copper wire, plastic

Andreas Fogarasi studied art at the Academy of Fine Arts in Vienna and architecture at the University of Applied Arts. Fogarasi’s work examines the cultural, political, and economic aspects of the built environment, with a particular focus on how urban spaces are transformed by the passage of time, ideologies, and modes of use. 
He frequently works with archival materials, documentation, and installation-based tools, through which he uncovers the intertwining narratives of the past and present. His video series “Kultur und Freizeit”, about Budapest’s community centres, won the Golden Lion for Best National Pavilion at the VeniceBiennale in 2007. Most of these cultural centres operated during the postStalinist era, and in addition to their function of ideological maintenance, they served as venues for various forms of social interaction, ranging from theatrical performances to communal TV viewings. At the same time, the community spaces of workers’ clubs became venues for underground culture between the 1960s 
and 1980s, only to lose their former significance after the fall of communism. Parallel to the shift in their original function, the social structure that had defined the relationship between culture and leisure up to that point also disintegrated.
The Haus der Begegnung (House of Encounters, Community Centre) project featured in the exhibition connects the fate of Hungarian cultural centres to the history of a public building in Vienna. Their Austrian/Viennese counterparts operated under the name Volkshochschule, or adult education centre, and the larger ones even had event halls. The community centre featured in the artist’s work is part of a large housing estate, the Per-Albin-Hansen-Siedlung.
The model and photographs do not merely capture the form of a building, but also its social role and ideological context. Originally built as a symbol of community gatherings and openness in the context of the modernist urban development efforts of the 1960s and 1970s, the building’s subsequent renovations, however, clearly illustrate how new political and economic interests have superseded the ideals of modernism. Fogarasi’s work is thus both a documentation and a critical reconstruction: the idealised perspective of the model is counterbalanced by the concrete, often fragmented imagery of the photographs. At the same time, the “Stripped series”—the so-called “material packages”—focuses on another, material aspect of urban transformations. Cladding, surfaces, and materials removed from various buildings appear as physical samples that bear the imprint of the aesthetic and economic decisions of their respective eras. While buildings are typically presented through plans, models, or renderings, Fogarasi presents them through their materiality: he takes samples from the building’s construction and cladding materials, assembles them into a relief, and binds them together with steel strips. He creates extracts and condensations, which he presents as a constructivist material package, as if he had simply peeled away the building’s bark, its skin. The “stripping” of materials is not only a physical but also a conceptual gesture: a metaphor for the city’s continuous rewriting, the cyclical nature of demolition and reconstruction.Fogarasi’s works do not offer a clear narrative; rather, they raise questions about how we relate to built heritage and the values that shape our public spaces. The two groups of works presented at the exhibition thus complement each other, reflecting on the interrelationships between memory, ideology, and materiality in contemporary urban space. (KJ)

Prior to his years of study in intermedia art, Marcell Esterházy lived in Paris for a short time: he explored photography through lengthy discussions with Lucien Hervé, the groundbreaking Hungarian-born architectural photographer, and it was through him that he “learned to see.” At the end of his studies at the University of Fine Arts in Budapest, he returned to France in 2003: this marked the beginning of his Marseille period, and one of his earliest works there was l.m.v.h. 2.0
In down-town Marseille, the Labourdette Towers, erected next to the old port, were built in a quite unusual manner in the early 1960s: a large-scale urban planning project aimed to revitalise the run-down, chaotic neighbourhood, though in 1967 a major archaeological discovery—crucial to the city’s history—prevented its completion. Even so, the three completed towers, which originally served hyper-functionality with a facade (white concrete) embodying a petit-bourgeois aesthetic, thoroughly reshaped the cityscape, just as Le Corbusier’s “Unité d’habitation,” erected a decade and a half earlier, had done (Hervé’s series on the building laid the foundation for his collaboration with Le Corbusier). 
Before the Labourdette Tower became yet another symbol of social impoverishment, an unusual story accompanied the tower’s past. Contrary to the social hierarchy, wealthier families initially lived on the lower floors, while less affluent families occupied the upper floors—a situation caused by the lack of an elevator. After the lift was installed, the residents gradually swapped places: the rich moved to the top, and the poor to the bottom. Esterházy explores this phenomenon in a video loop from the Ludwig Museum’s collection. Instead of moving images, he constructs the work’s structure from still frames, foreshadowing his working method through an exploration of the extreme possibilities of photographic technique. In this case, he weaves the threads of meaning anew through the meticulous deconstruction of a photograph. l.m.v.h. 2.0 does not aim for historical accuracy; rather, it suggests a documentary character attuned to the lens, yet ultimately reveals a personal interpretation. Elevating the photograph of the facade into a narrative space and time frame makes it possible to capture the social changes taking place within the building, thereby transforming the otherwise highly local story into a more universal narrative. In Esterházy’s later works as well, the visual unfolding of the work’s internal structure typically takes place in such a way that the subtle surface connections between hidden (historical,social) elements are barely perceptible to the viewer, and can only be interpreted as mere hints. Esterházy’s time in Marseille was spent exploring the city and experimenting. The Marseille landscape, as a multifaceted and contradictory environment, provided him with a constant source of inspiration. For example, he created models by casting Styrofoam packaging found on the street in concrete, through which a fictional urban fabric emerges. The model featured in the Polaroids and video titled Offret was the largest; it took nearly 
half a year to complete. However the original purpose of braking and smashing the cast was the opposite.
Esterházy intended the film, shot from a bird’s-eye view, for playback. The end result would have been a visualisation of the construction of a phalanstery: not necessarily Charles Fourier’s utopian self-sufficient housing estate, but rather a fictional urban space inspired by Marseille. The video, however, failed; it was technically unsuitable for playback, so it was boxed away as archival material until 2025, when it took on new meaning in the spirit of dystopia. The title references the original title of Andrei Tarkovsky’s film Sacrifice (1986), made in Sweden. During the filming of this apocalyptic movie, which foreshadows the dawn of a nuclear world war, a wooden house was supposed to burn down in a one-take scene, but the camera malfunctioned during the shoot: the crew spent two weeks rebuilding the entire set for another take. Marcell Esterházy’s video documents the sacrifice of his half a year’s work. (KB)

One such application is the book installation titled Handbook to the Stars (2012), which consists of an open-book structure with pages arranged in a map-like, interconnected pattern. “When I say I’m a photographer, I’m building walls around myself from my own perspective. Or I say I’m free, and I turn to whatever medium I want. If I want to, I’ll take photos my whole life, but I don’t want the medium to define me.” /Péter Puklus/

The other work on display by him is a documentary-style photographic inventory. The photographs are simple, analogue shots from the 1980s, intended for home use. The reason for their creation is rather grim: in the late 1980s, Puklus’s family received permission to resettle in Hungary from Romania, but they had to prepare a detailed inventory of all their personal belongings, documented with photographs, so that the Ministry of Culture could verify they were not taking with them any valuable items considered part of the cultural heritage and important to the Romanian state.

The photos preserved in the family photo album represent a bygone historical era, as well as material documents of the move, depicting everyday life in Romania and the necessities of daily living. The reign of party leader Ceaușescu, with the harsh nationalism of the Romanian variant of socialism, its plans to demolish villages, the hardships endured by the population, the strict surveillance of the people, and its attitude toward privacy, was a dystopia incarnate. On the display board, the photos are arranged in groups from floor to ceiling, ranging from home textiles to furniture and their contents: we see the rooms of the apartment from the entryway to the bathroom, family dinnerware, tablecloths and wall hangings, glassware, porcelain—in short, everything. The entire material environment of a family’s life is laid out in the images from the everyday life of (what was once) socialism: photos of objects—object photos—arranged in a map-like layout. Images that seem completely meaningless to the outside observer gain significance over time as the socio-political environment changes (in the exhibition: with the fall of once-dominant ideologies). They become historical documents, functioning as time capsules that link personal narratives to family history and collective memory. (KJ)

WALICZKY Tamás: Vision on Mong Kok East Footbridge, 2023
video, no sound; 1’13”

 
As with most large cities, Hong Kong’s cityscape is defined by its countless neon signs. The first coloured neon lights (gas discharge tubes) were made in the 1920s, but it was after the war, mainly in the 1950s and 1970s, that they really became widespread. At the height of the city’s neon sign era, some 80,000 illuminated surfaces covered Hong Kong’s buildings, strongly shaping the city’s visual landscape. The style of local neon signs, which are considered part of the cultural heritage, draws on several influences, including Chinese calligraphy, English characters, and Art Deco style elements. The colourful environment features prominently in Hong Kong’s film industry, as it inspired the makers of the film Blade Runner.
In 2010, the use of urban neon signs in Hong Kong was re-regulated and they were replaced with more energy-efficient and environmentally friendly LED lights. A mass modernization took place, during which many former neon signs were moved to museums. For safety reasons, many neon signs were dismantled and left without neon lights to comply with traffic safety and environmental regulations. Following the technological transition, experts and visual artists continue to keep the original, handcrafted neon technology alive.
Media artist Tamás Waliczky spent several years as a visiting professor at the School of Creative Media, City University of Hong Kong between 2016 and 2024.
 
“In Hong Kong, neon sign manufacturing is a local art industry. Hong Kongers remember the more famous neon signs. The city is full of neon lights, which are an integral part of the city’s image.
Once, when I was walking on a pedestrian bridge in eastern Mong Kok, I imagined that all the people walking on the bridge were wearing their own neon lights. It is not the environment that is filled with neon lights, but the people themselves who emit different colours of light. This idea was the basis for the animation.” WT
 
Hong Kong itself is a special place, a unique city: one of two special administrative regions of the People’s Republic of China (the other being Macau), it was a British colony and was returned to China in 1997. Based on the principle of “one country, two systems,” Beijing guarantees the preservation of these islands of capitalism within officially communist China for 50 years. The city-state has its own legal system, its own constitution and currency, and traffic still drives on the left. Developed Hong Kong has always been a major attraction in its own environment and attracts workers from further afield, from all over Southeast Asia. The city is a major hub, whose media and visual industries and education offer opportunities for a better life, which is why millions of people arrive and settle in the city’s real estate, in small, overcrowded apartments, for the duration of their studies or guest work. (KJ)
